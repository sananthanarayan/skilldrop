# Mulch type and summer soil moisture in Brackenford community gardens

Rosalind Achebe-Mortlake and Tobias Wren
Department of Urban Ecology, Brackenford Polytechnic (brackenford-poly.example)

## Abstract

Community gardeners in temperate cities increasingly face dry summers, and mulching is the most common water-saving advice they receive. We compared four surface treatments (bare soil, straw, wood chip and green-waste compost) in 48 raised beds across six community gardens in Brackenford over the summer of 2025. Volumetric soil moisture was logged hourly at 10 cm depth from June to August. Wood-chip mulch raised mean soil moisture by 23% relative to bare soil, and all three mulches reduced the number of days on which moisture fell below the wilting threshold. We recommend wood chip as the default mulch for allotment-style beds where it is locally available.

## 1. Introduction

Urban food growing has expanded rapidly over the last decade (Abernathy & Solberg, 2015; Calloway, 2018), and community gardens now supply a meaningful share of fresh vegetables for some low-income households (Dunmore et al., 2020). These gardens are often on compacted, low-organic-matter soils that dry quickly (Halvorsen, 2017).

Mulches reduce evaporation from the soil surface and moderate soil temperature (Quaresma, 2014). Straw and wood chip are widely recommended in gardening guidance, but field comparisons in small urban beds are scarce. Pemberton (2017) found that compost mulch improved moisture retention in rural market gardens, while Marchetti and Oyelaran (2021) reported that coarse wood chip outperformed finer materials in park plantings.

The aim of this study was to compare the effect of common, low-cost mulches on summer soil moisture in working community gardens.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Sites and design

Six community gardens across Brackenford were recruited through the city's allotment federation. In each garden, eight raised beds of similar size (1.2 m x 2.4 m) and soil were assigned at random to one of four treatments, giving two beds per treatment per garden and 48 beds in total (Figure 1).

### 2.2 Treatments

Mulches were applied in late May 2025 at a depth of 5 cm. Straw was locally sourced barley straw; wood chip was municipal arboricultural chip; compost was the city's certified green-waste compost. Bare beds were hand-weeded weekly.

### 2.3 Measurements

A capacitance soil-moisture sensor was installed at 10 cm depth in the centre of each bed and logged hourly from 1 June to 31 August 2025. Days below the wilting threshold were counted using the threshold for sandy loam given by Weatherby and Zhou (2022).

### 2.4 Analysis

Daily mean moisture per bed was analysed with a linear mixed-effects model with treatment as a fixed effect and garden as a random effect.

## 3. Results

Mean volumetric soil moisture differed between treatments (Table 1). All three mulches kept beds wetter than bare soil, with wood chip the wettest throughout the season (Figure 2).

Table 1. Mean volumetric soil moisture and days below wilting threshold, June to August 2025 (n = 12 beds per treatment).

| Treatment | Mean soil moisture (%) | Days below wilting threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Bare soil | 11.6 | 31 |
| Straw | 14.2 | 17 |
| Wood chip | 15.1 | 12 |
| Compost | 13.0 | 22 |

The difference between wood chip and bare soil was largest during the July heatwave, when bare beds fell below the wilting threshold on 19 consecutive days (Figure 3). Compost beds dried faster than the other mulched beds after rain.

## 4. Discussion

Our results agree with the field trials of Marchetti and Oyelaran (2021): coarse wood chip retained the most moisture. The weaker performance of compost may reflect its fine texture and the way it is incorporated into the soil surface by worms within weeks of application (Varga, 2021). Straw performed well but blew off two beds during a July storm, which gardeners reported as a practical drawback.

Wood chip is available free from the city's tree contractors, which makes it the most practical choice for most Brackenford gardens. Further work should test whether these effects persist over several seasons as mulches decompose.
