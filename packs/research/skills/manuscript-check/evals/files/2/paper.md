# Rooftop Forb Plantings Increase Solitary Bee Nesting in a Mid-Sized City

Running head: ROOFTOP FORBS AND SOLITARY BEES

## Abstract

Green roofs are often promoted as pollinator habitat, but most are planted with
sedum mats that offer little forage. We compared solitary bee nesting on 18 roofs in
Carrowmere: six sedum-only roofs, six mixed forb roofs and six bare control roofs.
Trap nests on forb roofs held three times as many brood cells as those on sedum roofs,
and nest occupancy rose with forb cover. We argue that planting choice, not roof
area, is the main lever for city planners.

## Introduction

Cities can support a surprising diversity of wild bees when floral resources and
nesting sites are both available (Quillfeather & Arslan, 2015). Cavity-nesting
species in particular readily colonise artificial trap nests placed in urban
settings (Achterberg & Nwosu, 2019), which makes them a practical study group for
comparing habitat treatments.

Green roofs have been proposed as stepping stones that connect fragmented urban
habitat (Haldane-Ruiz et al., 2016). Most installed roofs, however, are extensive
systems planted with drought-tolerant sedum, chosen for low maintenance rather than
ecological value (Okonkwo-Varga, 2019). Sedum flowers briefly and attracts mainly
generalist visitors (Lindqvst & Moreau, 2020).

Forb-rich roofs can extend the flowering season, but they need deeper substrate and
more care, and city planners have little evidence that the extra cost pays off for
pollinators (Tamberlane, 2021). Earlier roof surveys counted foraging bees rather than
nests, so it remains unclear whether roofs support reproduction or only feeding
(Pemberton-Shah, 2022).

Here we ask whether forb plantings increase solitary bee nesting on roofs compared
with sedum plantings and bare control roofs.

## Method

### Study sites

We selected 18 flat roofs in central Carrowmere, all between four and six storeys
high and at least 300 m apart (Figure 1). Six were sedum-only extensive roofs, six
had been converted to mixed native forb plantings at least three years before the
study, and six were bare gravel roofs used as controls.

### Trap nests

On each roof we placed four trap nests, each holding 40 reed and cardboard tubes of
three diameters, following the design of Vasquez-Tolland (2020). Nests were set out
in late March and collected in October.

### Floral surveys

Each month from April to September we recorded percentage forb cover in ten 1 m2
quadrats per roof, using the protocol of Delacroix-Hume and Petrov (2021).

### Analysis

We modelled brood cells per nest with a negative binomial mixed model, with roof type
and forb cover as fixed effects and roof as a random effect.

## Results

Across all roofs we recorded 2,184 brood cells from nine species. Mean brood cells per
nest were 31.2 on forb roofs, 10.4 on sedum roofs and 2.1 on control roofs (Table 1).

Brood cell counts rose with forb cover on both planted roof types (Figure 2). The
relationship held after removing the two roofs closest to the city park.

Mason bees made up most of the cells on forb roofs, while leafcutter bees were
recorded only on forb roofs with more than 30% cover (Figure 3).

## Discussion

Our results suggest that roofs can support solitary bee reproduction, not just
foraging, provided they are planted with forbs. This agrees with ground-level work
showing that nesting responds to floral cover within a few hundred metres
(Quillfeather & Arslan, 2015; Achterberg & Nwosu, 2019).

The low counts on sedum roofs match the view that sedum offers a short, narrow
flowering window (Lindqvst & Moreau, 2020). Planners weighing the higher cost of forb
roofs should set it against this difference in nesting (Pemberton-Shah, 2022).

Our study covered one season in one city. Longer studies should track whether
nesting on forb roofs persists as plantings age (Haldane-Ruiz et al., 2016).
